I · The rhythm of time

Anything that repeats can be a clock.

Around 1600, Galileo observed that a pendulum swings at nearly the same rate whether its arc is wide or narrow. Its period is governed primarily by its length. In 1656, Christiaan Huygens applied this principle to clockmaking, dramatically improving timekeeping accuracy, from minutes lost each day to mere seconds.

A metronome uses the same principle in reverse. Its pendulum carries a sliding weight that changes its effective length. Move the weight up and the pendulum slows; move it down and it speeds up.

A pendulum relies on gravity and must hang vertically, making it impractical for portable timekeeping. A watch solves this with a spring-driven balance wheel and hairspring, creating a compact oscillator that keeps time in any position.

II · The Anatomy of a Second

One second, inside a watch.

At 18,000 beats an hour, a watch divides each second into five.

Beat 1 of 5
  1. EnergyThe mainspring pushes constantly on the gear train. Nothing moves, because the last wheel is locked.
  2. Balance wheelSwings one way, then back: five swings a second. Each swing takes the same time, and that is the watch’s clock.
  3. EscapementAs the balance passes its centre it flips the pallet fork. One tooth of the escape wheel escapes, and gives the balance a push on its way.
  4. Gear trainEvery wheel behind the escape wheel jumps forward with it, then stops again.
  5. The handsThe seconds hand, on the fourth wheel, advances six degrees a second.

III · The Measure of Time

What, exactly, is a second?

Our understanding of a second has evolved alongside our ability to measure it. From the movement of the sun to the vibrations of an atom, each new definition has brought us closer to a more precise measure of time.

  1. Antiquity

    Solar Time

    Long before mechanical clocks, time was measured by the sun. Noon marked its highest point in the sky, and a day ran from one noon to the next. But solar days are not equal. Earth's tilted axis and elliptical orbit cause the apparent sun to move unevenly throughout the year.

  2. 1700s onward

    Mean Solar Time

    The sun's uneven motion made accurate timekeeping difficult. Astronomers solved this by calculating an imaginary sun that moved at a constant rate. A second became 1/86,400 of the mean solar day.

    As railways connected distant cities, standardized time became essential. Time zones followed, with international agreement reached in 1884.

  3. The Astronomer's Clock

    Sidereal Time

    There is another way to measure a day: by the stars rather than the sun. Earth completes one rotation relative to the distant stars in approximately 23 hours, 56 minutes and 4 seconds.

    The difference comes from Earth's orbit around the sun. Each day, our planet must turn a little further to bring the sun back to the same position in the sky.

  4. 1956 – 1967

    Ephemeris Time

    Even the mean solar day proved too irregular for increasingly precise science. Astronomers turned to Earth's orbit around the sun, defining the second using a fixed fraction of the tropical year of 1900.

    It was a more stable astronomical reference, but difficult to reproduce in practice. Atomic clocks were already pointing towards something better.

  5. 1955 – 1967

    Atomic Time

    Earth's rotation was no longer the most reliable clock available. In 1955, Louis Essen and Jack Parry developed a practical caesium atomic clock at Britain's National Physical Laboratory.

    Atoms offered something the planet could not: an extraordinarily consistent rhythm.

  6. Since 1967

    The SI Second

    Today, the second is defined by fixing the frequency of a particular transition in the caesium-133 atom at exactly 9,192,631,770 hertz.

    Count that many periods of the corresponding radiation, and one second has passed.

    Our most fundamental unit of time is now defined not by the movement of the heavens, but by the behaviour of an atom.

Keeping Atomic Time in Step with Earth

Atomic clocks keep remarkably steady time, but Earth does not rotate at a constant rate. Since 1972, leap seconds have occasionally been added to Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) to keep civil time aligned with the planet's rotation.

International Atomic Time (TAI) is currently 37 seconds ahead of UTC.

    Method and sources

    The solar clock uses the NOAA Global Monitoring Laboratory method, which follows Jean Meeus’s Astronomical Algorithms. NOAA describes that method as good to about a minute between 72° north and south, and less exact nearer the poles. Sunrise and sunset allow the standard 0.833° for refraction and the sun’s disc. The ring is drawn in five-minute steps and assumes one clock offset for the day, so on the two days a year the clocks change it is up to an hour out for part of the day.

    The moon’s phase uses the average length of a lunation, so it can be about a day out. The orbit is drawn as a circle. The mechanical watch in “One second” is a schematic, as on the Workbench.
