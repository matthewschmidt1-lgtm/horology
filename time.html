I · The Rhythm of Time

Anything that repeats can be a clock.

A pendulum’s beat depends on its length, hardly at all on how wide it swings. In 1656, Christiaan Huygens built that into a clock, and the best timekeepers went from losing minutes a day to losing seconds.

A metronome is a pendulum turned upside down. Slide the weight up and the beat slows. A watch swaps gravity for a hairspring, so it keeps time in any position.

    Method and sources

    The solar clock uses the NOAA Global Monitoring Laboratory method, which follows Jean Meeus’s Astronomical Algorithms. NOAA describes that method as good to about a minute between 72° north and south, and less exact nearer the poles. Sunrise and sunset allow the standard 0.833° for refraction and the sun’s disc. The ring is drawn in five-minute steps and assumes one clock offset for the day, so on the two days a year the clocks change it is up to an hour out for part of the day.

    The moon’s phase uses the average length of a lunation, so it can be about a day out. The orbit is drawn as a circle and the Earth’s day and night side as plain geometry, without twilight. The mechanical watch in “One tick” is a schematic, as on the Workbench. The small drawings beside the sounds are illustrations, not working mechanisms.

    The spans in “A universe of time” are rounded, commonly given figures: the age of the universe from measurements of the cosmic microwave background, the age of the Earth from radiometric dating, life from the oldest widely accepted fossils, our species from the oldest known Homo sapiens remains, and the mechanical clock from the first European examples of the late 1200s. The eight minutes of sunlight is the sun’s distance divided by the speed of light. The stars drawn behind the opening and that section are decoration, not a map of the sky.

    1. NOAA Global Monitoring Laboratory, Solar Calculator glossary and general solar position calculations
    2. Bureau International des Poids et Mesures, “SI base unit: second”
    3. National Physical Laboratory, “The second”
    4. Encyclopaedia Britannica, “Lengths of years and months”

    These figures, and the historical statements about Huygens and the repeater, are the commonly given accounts and were not individually checked for this page.
