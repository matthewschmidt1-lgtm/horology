What these mean
  • Civil time is what clocks show: mean time at your zone’s reference meridian, plus any daylight-saving hour.
  • Mean solar time follows an even, imaginary sun, at your own longitude.
  • Apparent solar time follows the real sun: what a sundial here would read. Solar noon, when the sun is highest, is almost never at twelve on the clock.
  • The equation of time is the gap between the last two. It swings between about 16 minutes ahead and 14 behind over a year.
  • These are calculated, not observed, and are good to about a minute. The method is at the foot of the page.

I · The Rhythm of Time

Anything that repeats can be a clock.

Around 1600, Galileo noticed that a pendulum takes almost the same time over a wide swing as a narrow one, so long as the swing is small. Its period depends on its length. In 1656, Christiaan Huygens built that into a clock, and the best timekeepers went from losing minutes a day to losing seconds.

A metronome is a pendulum turned upside down, its weight above the pivot. Slide the weight up and the beat slows; slide it down and it quickens. A watch swaps gravity for a hairspring, so its balance wheel keeps time in any position.

II · The Anatomy of a Second

One second, inside a watch.

At 18,000 beats an hour, a watch ticks five times a second. Two beats, there and back, make one full oscillation of the balance: 2.5 a second.

Beat 1 of 5
  1. EnergyThe mainspring pushes all the time. The escapement holds it back.
  2. Balance wheelSwings one way, then back. Each swing is one beat.
  3. EscapementAt centre, the balance flips the pallet fork. One tooth escapes and gives the balance a push.
  4. Gear trainEvery wheel jumps forward one step, then stops.
  5. The handsThe seconds hand moves six degrees a second, in five steps.

III · The Measure of Time

What, exactly, is a second?

The answer has moved from the sky to the atom, in four steps.

  1. Antiquity

    The Sun

    Our earliest clock. Noon is the sun at its highest, and a day runs from one noon to the next. But those days are not equal: Earth’s tilted axis and oval orbit make the sun run fast and slow through the year.

    See it on the solar clock

  2. 1700s onward

    The Clock

    Clocks cannot follow an uneven sun, so astronomers averaged it into an even, imaginary one. A second became 1/86,400 of that mean solar day. Railways then needed one time for many towns, and time zones followed, agreed internationally in 1884.

  3. 1955

    The Atom

    Earth’s rotation turned out to be unsteady too. In 1955, Louis Essen and Jack Parry ran a caesium clock at Britain’s National Physical Laboratory that kept better time than the planet.

    International Atomic Time-

  4. Since 1967

    The Second

    The second is now defined by an atom. The frequency of one transition of caesium-133 is fixed at exactly 9,192,631,770 hertz. Count that many cycles, and one second has passed.

Deeper: sidereal time, the astronomer’s clock

Measured against the stars, not the sun, Earth turns once in about 23 hours, 56 minutes and 4 seconds. The missing four minutes are Earth catching up with its own movement around the sun.

Local sidereal time-

Deeper: ephemeris time, the second from Earth’s orbit

Between the mean solar second and the atomic one came a short-lived third. In 1956 the second was redefined as a fixed fraction of the tropical year 1900, taken from Earth’s orbit, not its spin. It was steadier, but slow and awkward to measure, and the atomic second replaced it in 1967.

Leap seconds

Atomic clocks are steadier than the turning Earth, so since 1972 leap seconds have kept clock time (UTC) in step with the planet. Atomic time now runs 37 seconds ahead of UTC. In 2022 the world’s metrology body resolved to stop adding leap seconds by 2035.

We can count a second more finely than anything else we measure. We still cannot say what it is.

    Method and sources

    The solar clock uses the NOAA Global Monitoring Laboratory method, which follows Jean Meeus’s Astronomical Algorithms. NOAA describes that method as good to about a minute between 72° north and south, and less exact nearer the poles. Sunrise and sunset allow the standard 0.833° for refraction and the sun’s disc. The ring is drawn in five-minute steps and assumes one clock offset for the day, so on the two days a year the clocks change it is up to an hour out for part of the day.

    The moon’s phase uses the average length of a lunation, so it can be about a day out. The orbit is drawn as a circle and the Earth’s day and night side as plain geometry, without twilight. The mechanical watch in “One second” is a schematic, as on the Workbench. The small drawings beside the sounds are illustrations, not working mechanisms.

    The spans in “A universe of time” are rounded, commonly given figures: the age of the universe from measurements of the cosmic microwave background, the age of the Earth from radiometric dating, life from the oldest widely accepted fossils, our species from the oldest known Homo sapiens remains, and the mechanical clock from the first European examples of the late 1200s. The eight minutes of sunlight is the sun’s distance divided by the speed of light. These were not individually checked for this page. The stars drawn behind the opening and that section are decoration, not a map of the sky.
